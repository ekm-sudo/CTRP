# Test learned weights before smarter algorithms

The best challengers to the current taxpayer risk scorecard are glass-box models that learn from the same eight parameters. Exotic algorithms are not needed. In priority order, the shortlist is: **(1) a learned-weight logistic scorecard that predicts hits per check**, with an expert-anchored variant; **(2) a data-estimated, two-sided empirical Bayes shrinkage** to replace the fixed n/(n+10) credibility weight; **(3) a monotone-constrained additive boosting model** (Explainable Boosting Machine, or scikit-learn gradient boosting with monotonic constraints); **(4) a two-part expected-revenue model**; **(5) a directional peer-relative score** built on rule-based segments instead of HDBSCAN clusters; and **(6) network "exposure" features** that add guilt by association. The ordering rests on three findings. On tabular credit data, plain logistic regression, an EBM and XGBoost finished **within about 0.01 AUROC of each other**. The deployed customs system most like Uganda's (Senegal) improved by moving from weighted averages of fraud frequencies toward a logistic model on the same criteria. And with 87% of taxpayers holding fewer than 10 declarations, the way small samples are shrunk probably matters more than which algorithm sits on top. The neural customs models in the literature (DATE, GraphFC) report much higher lift. Those figures are declaration-level, though, and need GPU-scale data and embeddings, so they are neither comparable nor practical on a laptop with fixed-wording reasons. The biggest threat to a fair comparison is selection bias: any model trained on checked declarations partly re-learns the selectivity system that chose them. The evaluation protocol at the end is built around that problem. It uses rolling out-of-time snapshots, lift on hits per check, revenue capture, paired bootstrap intervals, lift within lane strata, coverage, stability and burden checks, and finally a live trial with a small random inspection share.

## Published customs lifts of 10x to 40x are not targets for this ranking

Start with what the published evidence measures, because it is easy to read the wrong benchmark. The best-known customs models come from the WCO BACUDA programme. DATE (KDD 2020) combines gradient-boosted tree paths with importer and HS embeddings. Trained on five years of import data with a 2.24% illicit rate, it reached **92.7% precision while inspecting only 1% of trade flows** ([KAIST, DATE abstract](https://koasas.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/277526?mode=full)). Against the base rate, that is roughly 41 times. GraphFC, its graph-neural successor, was tested on three real administrations. On the dataset with an 8.16% fraud rate, it reached **0.869 precision in the top 1% versus 0.671 for XGBoost** ([GraphFC, arXiv 2305.11377](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11377)), about 10.6 times the base rate. These figures look far above the scorecard's 4.6 / 4.5 / 4.1. The comparison does not hold, for three reasons. First, they score single declarations, not taxpayers six months ahead. Second, they were measured on transactions whose labels were known. Third, they rely on importer and HS embeddings and self-supervised pretraining over millions of transactions. The test sets ran from 1.4 million to 2.4 million transactions per country ([GraphFC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11377)). Uganda's setting is different: about 58,000 taxpayer rows per snapshot, and outcomes that exist only where a declaration was checked. The IRS audit-selection work dropped neural networks "due to limited training data" and used random forests instead ([Henderson et al., AAAI 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/25637/25409)). The same logic applies here with more force.

The closer analogue is Senegal's SAGAR system, built in house by the customs administration. Version 1.0 was a **weighted average of historical fraud frequencies** per importer, origin, HS code, regime and shipping agent. Version 2.0 was planned as a **logistic regression on those same frequency criteria** ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)). In back-tests against the existing selectivity system, it **cut inspections by 61% while still capturing 28 of 34 fraud cases** in Q1 2011. In Q2 2011, it cut inspections by 72% and captured 24 of 45 cases. The fraud rate among inspected declarations rose from 0.93% to 1.72% ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)). Structurally, URA's scorecard sits where SAGAR 1.0 sat: directional parameters, expert weights, an additive score. The natural next step is the one Senegal planned, which is to keep the parameters and let the outcomes set the weights.

Evidence from neighbouring fields points the same way. On Lending Club credit data with a time-based split, the three model families scored almost identically. **Logistic regression reached AUROC 0.6653, XGBoost 0.6687 and an EBM 0.6744**. The EBM kept its performance even after the feature set was cut by 88.5% ([Schwartz, Wang, Fang, arXiv 2509.11389](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.11389)). In Italian tax audits, the gain from machine learning came from a better choice of whom to audit at a fixed budget. **Replacing the 10% least productive audits with model-selected taxpayers raised detected evasion by 38%** ([NBER w30777](https://nber.org/papers/w30777)). Taken together, the lesson is that the large wins come from the target definition, the treatment of sparse evidence and the selection design. The extra layers of a model add little by comparison. That is why the shortlist below starts with models that change one of those things at a time.

## Selection bias decides which challenger can win fairly

The outcome is defined as checked (red or yellow) declarations that ended in a top-up. That definition is honest, because it is conditional on inspection. But the checks were chosen by the selectivity system, and that system probably uses signals close to the scorecard's own parameters. Three consequences follow, and each one shapes the shortlist.

The first consequence is that **supervised challengers partly learn the selectivity system rather than taxpayer behaviour**. The India GST field experiment shows how far this can go. A random forest found non-existent firms with a **53% field hit rate, against 38% for officials' rules of thumb and 4% for random inspection**. Yet model-driven inspections did not significantly raise cancellations compared with a randomised holdout. The model had learned to find firms "already on a trajectory toward cancellation" ([Barwahwala et al., NBER w32705](https://are.berkeley.edu/~aprajit/w32705.pdf)). The expert scorecard is less exposed to this circularity, because nobody fitted its weights to past selectivity outcomes. That is a real argument for the incumbent, and a challenger has to beat it on evaluation slices that selectivity did not drive.

The second consequence is **range restriction**. Suppose a parameter already sends declarations to red or yellow. Then, among checked declarations, taxpayers vary less on that parameter, and its learned coefficient is pulled toward zero. So a small learned weight does not prove an expert-important parameter is useless. It may only mean that the selectivity system already acted on it. This is why the shortlist favours an expert-anchored learned scorecard over a pure data fit.

The third consequence is that **evaluating only on checked cases overstates performance and can reverse model rankings**. In credit reject-inference experiments, evaluating on accepted applicants only estimated a bad rate of about 0.014 when the true rate was 0.210. On a real dataset with a randomised acceptance sample, the error in estimated AUC fell from 0.1234 (accepts only) to 0.0111 ([Kozodoi et al., arXiv 2407.13009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13009)). The IRS trains audit-selection models on its stratified random National Research Program audits rather than on operational audits. The reason given is that "when a return is selected for OP audit, the IRS has reason to believe that the return represents a misreport" ([Black et al., FAccT 2022](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.09875)). Senegal kept a random-selection share for the same reasons: to stop operators and officers gaming published criteria, and because its early models were "fairly unreliable" ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)). Customs simulations on three countries' data reached the same conclusion. A **hybrid of likely-fraud and uncertain items eventually outperforms exploitation-only selection**, because trade patterns drift ([Kim et al., arXiv 2010.14282](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14282)).

In practice, this means the question is not only which challenger scores the highest lift. It is which challenger keeps its lift on the cases that selectivity did not pre-sort. The protocol section turns that into concrete checks.

## Peer groups and anomaly scores failed for a known reason

The team found that HDBSCAN peer groups (77% noise) and a raw-feature Isolation Forest added no lift. The literature predicts both results. Neither failure rules out peer comparison altogether.

Isolation Forest finds global low-density points. A benchmark of 33 unsupervised detectors on 52 real tabular datasets found that **Isolation Forest variants win on global anomalies, kNN wins on local anomalies (17 of 18 comparisons), and deep methods underperform** on tabular data ([Bouman, Bukhsh, Heskes, JMLR 2024](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume25/23-0570/23-0570.pdf)). Station spread and trading structure describe who a taxpayer is, not what it did wrong. A general merchant trading many HS headings through many stations is globally rare without being risky. So a raw-feature Isolation Forest ranks "unusual" rather than "non-compliant". Density clustering fails on trade data for a related reason: trade is a continuum with piles driven by declaration counts, not a set of dense islands. That is why most points end up labelled as noise. Tax administrations that benchmark against peers use **rule-based segments**, not density clusters. The ATO builds industry ratio ranges per turnover band, and it says plainly that "We never use the benchmarks in isolation" ([ATO](https://www.ato.gov.au/Business/Small-business-benchmarks/How-we-use-business-performance-benchmarks/)). The Belgian VAT study ran its detectors separately within each of 10 sectors ([Vanhoeyveld et al. 2020](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/6255c9/163682_2020_10_31.pdf)).

The most useful unsupervised result is also the plainest. In that Belgian study, lift over random ran from **about 5 to 100 times** across sectors. The authors still recommended their simple Baseline, a sum of sector-normalised ratio deviations, over the fancier kNN method. They called it "more intuitive, more comprehensible and [with] much lower computational cost". The advanced methods beat the Baseline in only 22 of 30 sector and capacity combinations ([Vanhoeyveld et al. 2020](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/6255c9/163682_2020_10_31.pdf)). Two lessons transfer to URA. First, compare risk features against peers in one direction only: being worse than peers counts, being unusual does not. Second, use trading structure to define the peers, not as a risk signal. Bolton and Hand style peer group analysis does this per entity. Each account's peers are the accounts with similar past behaviour, and only later divergence from them is flagged ([Weston, Hand et al. 2008](https://cer.business-school.ed.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/55/2017/03/weston-hand-whitrow.pdf)). Every taxpayer gets peers by construction, so no noise class appears.

## A ranked shortlist of six challengers

The table gives the ranking at a glance. The subsections then explain each challenger in a fixed order: what it is, why it is strong, how to build it on this data, what evidence supports it, and what can go wrong. The rank combines expected lift, strength of evidence, explainability for officers, and how cleanly the comparison isolates one design change.

| Rank | Challenger | What it changes versus the scorecard | Main evidence | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Learned-weight logistic scorecard (plus expert-anchored variant) | Weights learned from outcomes; same parameters and reason format | Senegal SAGAR 2.0 path; LR within 0.01 AUROC of boosting | Weights absorb selectivity bias |
| 2 | Empirical Bayes shrinkage upgrade | Data-estimated, two-sided shrinkage toward peer means | Bühlmann credibility; shrinkage of rate statistics | Small-n traders move up the ranking |
| 3 | Monotone additive boosting (EBM or HistGradientBoosting) | Non-linear thresholds and a few interactions | EBM matched XGBoost on credit data | Jagged shapes on sparse events |
| 4 | Two-part expected-revenue model | Ranks by expected top-up amount, not frequency | IRS regression vs classification; DATE/GraphFC dual objectives | Shifts burden toward large traders |
| 5 | Directional peer-relative score on rule-based segments | Unsupervised, one-sided, peer-normalised | Belgian VAT Baseline; ATO benchmarks | Lower lift than supervised models |
| 6 | Network exposure features | Guilt by association via HS-origin (later broker) links | GOTCHA +55%; Bulgaria VAT network | Leakage if built without time cut-offs |

### Rank 1: a learned-weight logistic scorecard that predicts hits per check

**What it is.** It is a logistic regression on the same eight parameters, cut into officer-recognisable bands. Its points come from coefficients fitted to outcomes, not from expert likelihood-times-impact weights. In form it is a credit-style points scorecard, so officers keep the same artefacts: named parameters, bands, points per band, and fixed reason wording.

**Why it is the strongest challenger.** It changes exactly one thing, the weights, so any difference in lift can be traced to whether the expert weights were right. It is the documented upgrade path of the most comparable deployed customs system ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)). And on tabular data, logistic regression came within about 0.01 AUROC of boosted trees ([arXiv 2509.11389](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.11389)), so little accuracy is lost by staying linear. Even if it does not win, it tells the team which expert weights the outcomes disagree with. That is useful on its own.

**How to build it, step by step.** The steps below are the researchers' method design, not a published recipe.

First, set the target to hits per check, because that is what the lift metric rewards. For each taxpayer with at least one checked declaration in the outcome window, let m be the number of checks and y the number that ended in a top-up. Create two weighted rows per taxpayer: a "hit" row with weight y and a "no hit" row with weight m minus y. Fit scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` with `sample_weight`. Alternatively, fit `statsmodels` GLM with a Binomial family and a two-column outcome (y, m minus y). Do not use "any hit" as the target, because it mixes risk with how often a taxpayer was checked.

Second, bin each parameter. Start from the scorecard's existing concern levels and maxima, so the bands mean something to officers. Merge bins that hold too few hits, and keep a separate "too few declarations" bin rather than imputing. Pool several monthly or semi-annual snapshots to gain events, using time-ordered splits.

Third, enforce monotone risk. Higher top-up share, amendment share or below-benchmark price share must never lower the score. Drop or merge any bin whose weight-of-evidence goes the wrong way. A non-monotone pattern that survives coarse binning is a finding to investigate, not something to hide.

Fourth, handle correlated parameters. Top-up share, severe top-ups and top-ups as a share of final tax overlap heavily. Use L2 regularisation, or fold them into one "top-up behaviour" characteristic with sub-bands, so the model does not split their credit at random.

Fifth, scale the coefficients to points, using the standard points-to-double-the-odds formula. Rank each taxpayer's parameters by points above the lowest-risk band, and map the top two to four onto fixed reason texts.

Sixth, fit an **expert-anchored variant** next to the pure fit. Add the current expert score as an unpenalised input and ridge-penalise the individual parameters. Each parameter's total weight then equals c times its expert weight plus a deviation, and the penalty shrinks that deviation toward zero. A large penalty returns the expert scorecard. A zero penalty returns the pure data model. Choose the penalty by out-of-time lift. `statsmodels`, or a short `scipy.optimize` routine, handles this directly. This variant is the right answer to range restriction: the data move a weight only where the evidence is strong.

**Evidence.** The supporting evidence is Senegal's frequency-score results and its logistic upgrade path ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)), plus the near-parity of logistic, EBM and XGBoost on tabular credit data ([arXiv 2509.11389](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.11389)). No published study was found that benchmarks a learned scorecard against a hand-weighted expert scorecard in customs. URA's own back-test would be the first such evidence.

**Risks.** Learned weights inherit selectivity bias, and they shrink toward zero for parameters that selectivity already uses. Bins with few events produce unstable weights. And the top 1% is only about 580 taxpayers, of whom only those with checks count, so lift at that cut-off is noisy.

### Rank 2: empirical Bayes shrinkage estimated from the data

**What it is.** It replaces the fixed one-way credibility weight n/(n+10) with shrinkage whose strength is estimated from the data for each parameter. Rates are pulled toward a peer-group mean in both directions. A conservative lower-bound version is also offered for officer wording.

**Why it is strong.** With 87% of taxpayers below 10 declarations, the shrinkage rule decides most of the ranking. The current rule is a special case of Bühlmann credibility, Z = n/(n+K), where K is the ratio of within-taxpayer noise to true between-taxpayer variation and can be estimated from the data ([SOA STAM note 23-18](https://www.soa.org/Files/Edu/2018/2018-stam-23-18.pdf)). Fixing K at 10 for every parameter assumes that amendments, severe top-ups and under-benchmark pricing are all equally noisy, which is unlikely. One-way shrinkage also leaves a taxpayer with 0 top-ups in 2 declarations at zero, below the population mean. That systematically ranks newcomers as safe. Research on rate statistics shows that pulling small-population rates toward a reference **outperforms the raw estimate in global risk**, because small samples inflate extreme rankings ([Holsbø & Perduca, arXiv 1810.07654](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1810.07654)).

**How to build it, step by step.** First, for each rate parameter, fit a beta-binomial model to all taxpayers' (successes x, declarations n). Use `scipy.stats.betabinom.logpmf` with `scipy.optimize`, or the Bühlmann-Straub moment estimators. This takes seconds on 58,000 rows, and K equals a plus b. Second, compute the two-sided shrunk rate, (x + a)/(n + a + b). Third, replace the single population mean with a peer-group mean: the dominant HS section by size band, shrunk toward the national mean if the group is small. A 20% top-up rate can be ordinary in one HS section and alarming in another. Fourth, if policy wants "do not flag on thin evidence", also compute the 10th percentile of each taxpayer's posterior. That figure is low whenever evidence is thin and high only when evidence is strong. It also gives an officer-ready sentence: "at least X% of declarations topped up, with 90% confidence". Fifth, run the existing scorecard unchanged on these inputs, so the comparison isolates the effect of shrinkage. Then reuse the shrunk rates, plus log(n), as inputs to challengers 1, 3 and 4.

**Evidence.** The credibility theory is standard and well documented ([SOA STAM note](https://www.soa.org/Files/Edu/2018/2018-stam-23-18.pdf)), and the advantage of shrinkage for small-sample rate rankings is shown in simulation ([arXiv 1810.07654](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1810.07654)). Senegal faced the same small-count problem with importer fraud frequencies, but the paper does not describe any shrinkage. It handled occasional operators on a separate track and inspected new operators compulsorily ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)). No customs source was found that estimates K from data. This is a gap URA's back-test can fill.

**Risks.** Two-sided shrinkage lifts thin-history taxpayers toward their peer mean. That can crowd the top ranks with taxpayers who have few checks, which lowers coverage. Evaluate the thin and thick segments separately, and compare the posterior-mean and lower-bound versions directly.

### Rank 3: monotone-constrained additive boosting

**What it is.** It is a gradient-boosted model that stays additive, or nearly so, and is forced to respect the scorecard's risk directions. There are two interchangeable builds. One is InterpretML's `ExplainableBoostingClassifier`: one learned shape per feature plus a handful of pairwise terms ([Nori et al., arXiv 1909.09223](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09223)). The other is scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` with `monotonic_cst` set to +1 on directional features, and shallow trees or `interaction_cst` to limit interactions.

**Why it is strong.** It can find the thresholds a linear scorecard misses: where risk jumps, where it flattens, and whether, say, under-benchmark pricing matters only alongside high top-up share. Because it is additive, each taxpayer's score still splits exactly into per-feature contributions, and the top three contributions become reason codes. The monotonic constraints matter for two reasons. They keep explanations consistent with the directional scorecard. And they stop the model from fitting artefacts of selection in sparse ranges. The IRS audit research also recommended considering monotonicity constraints ([Black et al., FAccT 2022](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.09875)).

**How to build it, step by step.** First, use the same expanded hit and no-hit rows with sample weights as in Rank 1, and the shrunk inputs from Rank 2. Second, fit a pure additive model first (no interactions). Add 5 to 10 pairs only if they raise out-of-time lift. Third, set a strong minimum number of samples per leaf, because the events are sparse. Fourth, for an exposure-aware count version, fit `HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="poisson")` on y/m with `sample_weight=m`. This is the boosted equivalent of a Poisson model with an exposure offset. Fifth, convert each learned shape into 3 to 5 bands at its visible jumps, round the contributions to points, and refit. The result is a "scorecard learned by a GAM" that officers can read in the existing format.

**Evidence.** On Lending Club data, the EBM matched or slightly beat XGBoost and logistic regression ([arXiv 2509.11389](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.11389)). InterpretML's authors report EBM accuracy close to many black-box models ([arXiv 1909.09223](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09223)). China Customs found that boosting C5.0 trees raised test AUC from 0.836 to 0.982 ([Zhou, World Customs Journal 2019](https://worldcustomsjournal.org/Archives/Volume%2013,%20Number%202%20(Sep%202019)/1886%2002%20WCJ%20v13n2%20Xin%20Zhou.pdf)). That study used a random split, though, so trader leakage likely inflates the figure. No customs deployment of EBM or monotone boosting was found.

**Risks.** Shapes over sparse ranges will be jagged without regularisation. The extra officer review effort is justified only if it beats Rank 1 by more than the width of the bootstrap interval. SHAP explanations of deeper trees are less stable as fixed wording than exact additive contributions, so keep the model additive.

### Rank 4: a two-part expected-revenue model

**What it is.** It ranks taxpayers by expected top-up value rather than top-up frequency. Part one is the hits-per-check probability from Rank 1 or Rank 3. Part two is a regression of the top-up amount given a hit, for example a gamma GLM or a boosted regressor on log amount. The ranking score is the product of the two, optionally multiplied by the expected number of checks.

**Why it is strong.** The current outcome counts hits, so a model can win on hit rate by finding many small top-ups. The IRS research found that switching from a classification target (whether a return misreports) to a regression target (how much) **"obtains significantly more revenue than even unconstrained classification"**. Flexible classifiers recovered roughly **3 to 4 billion dollars versus about 6 billion** for a simpler model ([Black et al., FAccT 2022](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.09875)). The customs research models are built around the same idea: DATE and GraphFC both predict fraud and revenue together and rank by revenue impact ([KAIST DATE](https://koasas.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/277526?mode=full); [GraphFC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11377)). Two of the scorecard's parameters (severe top-ups, top-ups as a share of final tax) already measure impact, so this challenger tests whether impact is better handled as a separate prediction.

**How to build it.** Reuse the Rank 1 or Rank 3 probability. Fit the amount model only on taxpayers with hits, using the same shrunk inputs. Score revenue@k (the share of total top-up value captured in the top k) alongside lift. Then compare the expert impact ratings with the learned amount effects, separately from comparing likelihood ratings with learned hit effects. That split is cleaner than comparing the combined likelihood-times-impact weight.

**Evidence.** The IRS findings above, and the dual-objective customs models. A synthetic-data study reported that inspecting 10% recovered 88.63% of revenue at risk ([Sci Rep 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-33382-z)). Its data were generated synthetically with a 78% fraud share, so it should not be treated as evidence for real-world performance.

**Risks.** Revenue ranking concentrates checks on large traders. The IRS work warns that "a narrow focus on return-on-investment can seriously undermine vertical equity goals" ([Black et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.09875)). Amount models are also noisy with few hits. Present this model as a second ranking axis, not a replacement for the hit-rate score, unless management sets revenue as the objective.

### Rank 5: a directional peer-relative score on rule-based segments

**What it is.** It is an unsupervised, additive score in the style of the Belgian Baseline. Each taxpayer's shrunk risk parameters are expressed as percentiles within its peer segment. Only worse-than-peer deviations count, and they are summed.

**Why it is strong.** It is the one challenger whose weights are not fitted to selectivity-driven outcomes. That makes it a useful check on circularity: high precision combined with low overlap with the supervised models is where it adds value. It also fixes the cause of the HDBSCAN failure. Segments are built by rule, every taxpayer gets peers, and trading structure defines the peer group rather than adding risk points. Its explanations are natural for officers: "top-up share is in the worst 5% of importers of HS chapter X of similar size".

**How to build it, step by step.** First, define segments as the dominant HS chapter (by value share) crossed with a size band, with a minimum segment size and fallback to the parent HS section. Second, as a second variant, give each taxpayer its k nearest neighbours (for example 50 to 200) in log-scaled trading-structure space, Bolton and Hand style. Third, compute each risk parameter's percentile within the peer group, using the shrunk rates from Rank 2, with median-based statistics so a segment that under-declares as a whole does not shift its own benchmark. Fourth, score only the upper tail, and sum with equal weights or the expert weights. Fifth, anchor price features to external HS benchmarks rather than peer medians, which limits contamination by peers.

**Evidence.** In the Belgian VAT study, lift over random ran from about 5 to 100 times, and the simple Baseline was recommended over LOF and kNN ([Vanhoeyveld et al. 2020](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/6255c9/163682_2020_10_31.pdf)). ATO industry benchmarks are used as one input among several ([ATO](https://www.ato.gov.au/Business/Small-business-benchmarks/How-we-use-business-performance-benchmarks/)). Peer group analysis has been tested on real card fraud data ([Weston, Hand et al. 2008](https://cer.business-school.ed.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/55/2017/03/weston-hand-whitrow.pdf)). No study was found that compares one-sided and two-sided scoring on customs outcomes.

**Risks.** Unsupervised scores usually trail supervised ones on precision, and fraudsters can "approximate legal types of conduct" ([Vanhoeyveld et al. 2020](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/6255c9/163682_2020_10_31.pdf)). If it loses on lift, consider keeping it as an independent second opinion, judged on its overlap with the champion and on precision in a random slice.

### Rank 6: network exposure features from shared HS-origin and broker links

**What it is.** It is a set of features measuring how much of a taxpayer's trade runs through product-origin combinations (and later brokers or suppliers) where other importers have been topped up. Optionally, it adds a propagation score that spreads known hits across an importer-to-resource graph with time decay.

**Why it is strong.** It brings in information the eight parameters lack. A newcomer with no history of its own still has trading links, so this directly helps the 87% thin-history majority. GraphFC's importer and HS "virtual nodes" do the same job of borrowing strength, but at neural scale ([GraphFC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11377)).

**How to build it.** First, build a bipartite graph linking each importer to the HS heading, origin, station and (if recorded) supplier on its declarations. Second, for each importer, compute the value-weighted share of its HS-origin pairs whose shrunk top-up rate among other importers is high. Use only outcomes dated before the snapshot, and exclude the importer's own record. Third, add this as one feature to Ranks 1 and 3, with a fixed reason such as "trades mainly in product-origin lines with high top-up rates". Fourth, once broker IDs are available, add importer-to-broker links, which mirror the shared-resource links that drove the Belgian result below. Graph neural networks are not needed at 58,000 nodes. The real-data studies below used propagation scores and aggregated neighbour features.

**Evidence.** The Belgian social security study GOTCHA! detected **up to 55% more fraudsters in the top 100** than intrinsic features alone, in an out-of-time test, by propagating fraud through a time-weighted company-resource graph ([Van Vlasselaer et al., Management Science 2017](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/lakoglu/pubs/16-ManagmntScience-Gotcha.pdf)). In Bulgarian VAT, a network-informed model on a laptop flagged 200 suspects that yielded 100 confirmed new high-risk taxpayers, with 40 of the top 50 being new fraud cases ([Alexopoulos et al., arXiv 2106.14005](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.14005v3)).

**Risks.** Leakage is the main danger: neighbour rates must be computed from outcomes dated before the snapshot and must exclude the taxpayer itself. Guilt by association can also penalise honest traders in a high-risk commodity line, which is a burden question for the protocol. Rank it last because it is a feature addition, not a standalone model, and its customs evidence is indirect.

### What to leave off the list, and why

Five approaches are deprioritised, each for a specific reason. **DATE and GraphFC** need declaration-level embeddings, millions of transactions and GPU-friendly training, and their attention-based explanations do not produce fixed wording. **Isolation Forest, LOF or autoencoders on raw features** repeat the team's own negative result. If tried again, they should run only on directional, peer-normalised risk parameters, because deep detectors underperform on tabular data ([JMLR 2024](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume25/23-0570/23-0570.pdf)). **Learning-to-rank losses** (LambdaRank, pairwise) have no customs evidence of beating a well-calibrated classifier ranked by score. **Survival models on calendar time** mostly measure how often a taxpayer is checked, because a top-up can only happen on a checked declaration. A discrete hazard per checked declaration reduces to Rank 1 with a recency feature. **Positive-unlabelled learning** has tax applications in China, such as RR-PU ([AAAI 2024](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/28665)), but no customs importer-level results were found. It is better treated as a later experiment than as a shortlist entry. The recency, frequency and trend features that survival thinking suggests (months since last top-up, recent versus earlier shrunk rate) are worth testing as additions to Ranks 1 and 3.

## An evaluation protocol that keeps the comparison fair

The protocol has ten steps. Each step answers one way a comparison could mislead, and the reason is given with the step.

**Step 1: fix one target and one unit for every model.** Score taxpayers. Measure lift as the hit rate among checked declarations of the top-k taxpayers divided by the overall hit rate. Use exactly the definition behind the 4.6 / 4.5 / 4.1 figures, so the incumbent's numbers carry over. Add revenue@k as a second axis, because Black et al. showed that frequency and magnitude targets select different taxpayers ([FAccT 2022](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.09875)).

**Step 2: rebuild features "as of" each snapshot.** Top-ups, amendments and post-clearance adjustments can be recorded months after the declaration date. Every feature must therefore use only records dated before the snapshot. Today's view of past declarations leaks future information into the features.

**Step 3: train forward in time only.** For a test snapshot at time t, train only on snapshots whose six-month outcome window closed before t. Choose hyperparameters, bins and feature sets on the early snapshots, and seal the last one or two as an untouched holdout. Customs research validates forward in time because trade patterns drift ([Kim et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.14282)), and Senegal recalibrated quarterly on rolling four-quarter windows ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf)). Consecutive snapshots with 24-month windows share most of their history, so read the results as a time series, not as independent repeats.

**Step 4: always include the two reference models.** Run past top-up ratio alone and the current scorecard on every snapshot and slice. These references show where each challenger adds value. The scorecard's margin over past top-up ratio is wide at 1% (4.6 versus 2.9) and narrow at 10% (4.1 versus 3.6), so a challenger can win at one cut-off and not another.

**Step 5: report coverage next to every lift.** For each model and each k, report the share of its top-k taxpayers who had at least one checked declaration in the outcome window. Unchecked taxpayers cannot be scored. Dropping them silently favours models that agree with selectivity, and a model whose top 1% is mostly unchecked has not been tested at 1%.

**Step 6: put a confidence interval on every difference with a paired taxpayer bootstrap.** Within each snapshot, resample taxpayers with replacement 1,000 to 2,000 times. Recompute every model's lift and revenue@k on the same resample, and take the percentile interval of the difference (challenger minus scorecard). Pairing makes the comparison much tighter than comparing two separate intervals. Resampling taxpayers rather than declarations respects the fact that one importer's declarations are correlated. This is standard practice rather than a customs-specific rule. Monte Carlo approaches to evaluation uncertainty are used in the scorecard literature ([Du Pisanie et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01227); [Kozodoi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13009)).

**Step 7: test whether a challenger beats selectivity, not just agrees with it.** This is the core defence against selection bias. First, report each model's correlation with the selectivity score or lane. Second, report lift within lane strata: red only, then yellow only. That asks whether the model ranks better among cases selectivity treated alike. Third, assemble any less-biased slices that exist internally: random or compulsory inspections, post-clearance audit results on green-lane declarations (the analogue of Italy's later audits, [NBER w30777](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30777/w30777.pdf)), and variation in check intensity across stations or campaigns. Whether URA's selectivity has a random component is an internal question the notes could not answer. Fourth, as sensitivity analyses only, re-weight checked declarations by the inverse of their estimated probability of being checked, and run a Kozodoi-style pseudo-label evaluation over the full population ([Kozodoi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13009)). Present both alongside, never instead of, an unbiased slice, because inverse weighting fails when selection uses information that is not recorded, such as tip-offs ([Lakkaraju et al., KDD 2017](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/kdd17-selective.pdf)).

**Step 8: check stability, burden and calibration.** For stability, measure top-k overlap between consecutive snapshots and the share of new entrants. Officers lose trust if rankings jump, but very high stability combined with falling lift means the model reacts too slowly. Track PSI on scores and features. Treat the usual 0.1 / 0.25 thresholds as rough guides, because PSI critical values depend on sample size and binning ([Du Pisanie et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01227)). For burden, compare who each model's top-k contains by size, station, sector and new versus established status. A small hit-rate gain that shifts checks onto small or new traders is the customs version of the IRS equity finding ([Black et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.09875)). For calibration, show a predicted-versus-observed table by decile. Report thin-history (fewer than 10 declarations) and well-observed taxpayers separately throughout, as Senegal did with its separate track for occasional importers.

**Step 9: review reasons with officers, blind to model identity.** Give officers a sample of top-ranked taxpayers, including top-ranked non-hits, with each model's fixed-wording reasons and the model names hidden. Ask whether each reason is credible and useful. This separates "unusual but legitimate" from "not yet examined". It is evidence about explainability and operational fit, not about accuracy.

**Step 10: decide by a pre-written rule, then confirm live.** Write the decision rule before seeing the results. A reasonable version: a challenger displaces or joins the champion only if its paired-bootstrap interval for the lift difference at the operational k excludes zero in most snapshots, including the sealed holdout; it holds up within lane strata and on any unbiased slice; and it passes the stability, burden and reason checks. With six overlapping snapshots, six wins out of six corresponds to about p = 0.03 one-sided, so consistency is the practical test, not formal significance. Then run a live champion-challenger period. Let a small share of checks be allocated by the challenger, and protect a small random share. The random share also provides the exploration that Senegal, the IRS and the BACUDA simulations each found necessary ([FERDI P46](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-pBwYKbpMwnFKeaKVHJEPzwR5/ferdi-p46-inspecting-less-to-inspect-better-the-use-of-data-mining-for-risk.pdf); [Henderson et al., AAAI 2023](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/25637/25409); [Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14282)). Denmark is randomising audits across ML risk strata for exactly this reason ([AEA RCT 10813](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/10813)). Document each model in an SR 11-7 style pack, with someone who did not build it acting as the "effective challenge" ([ModelOp summary of SR 11-7](https://modelop.com/ai-governance/ai-regulations-standards/sr-11-7)).

## Conclusion

The negative results the team already has are informative. Peer groups and anomaly scores failed because they measured unusualness on features that describe identity, not because peer comparison is a dead end. The fix is to make peers a frame of reference and risk directional. More broadly, the evidence suggests the scorecard's remaining headroom lies in three places: how thin evidence is shrunk, whether the expert weights match outcomes, and whether frequency or value is the right target. Algorithmic sophistication is a smaller lever. A sequence that changes one of these at a time (shrinkage, then learned weights, then non-linear shapes, then the revenue target) gives an attributable answer to "what actually improved lift", which a single complex model cannot.

The deeper implication concerns data rather than models. Without a protected random or post-clearance slice, no offline back-test can fully separate "this model finds non-compliance" from "this model agrees with selectivity", and the India GST experiment shows the two can diverge sharply. The most valuable output of this challenger exercise is therefore an evidence base: a learned-versus-expert weight comparison officers can debate, and a case for a small exploration share that would make every future model comparison trustworthy. A winning model is a secondary outcome.
